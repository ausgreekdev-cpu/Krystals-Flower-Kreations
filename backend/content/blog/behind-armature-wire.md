## A flower without a spine

People assume paper flowers are all paper. They are not. Almost every piece that leaves my Perth studio has a skeleton inside it — armature wire — and that wire is what makes a bloom nod, lean and hold its head the way a real stem does.

Take the wire out and you have a limp cut-out. Put the right wire in and the flower has posture. Today, behind the scenes on how that actually works.

## What armature wire is

Armature wire is the same stuff used inside clay sculptures and stop-motion puppets: annealed or galvanised steel wire, sold by gauge. I keep four sizes on the bench:

- **18-gauge (1.2mm):** the workhorse. Stems for most bouquets, the trunk of small sculptures.
- **20-gauge (0.9mm):** per-stem wire inside leaves and heavier petals.
- **24-gauge (0.5mm):** fine branching — wattle twigs, banksia filaments.
- **26-gauge (0.45mm):** internal petal ribbing, invisible support for wide open faces.

Galvanised means a zinc coating: it will not rust if it ever meets Perth's coastal air or the condensation from a vase. Annealed means heat-softened — it bends smoothly without springing back. For floristry I buy galvanised and accept a slightly stiffer hand.

## How the wire becomes a stem

Every paper flower follows the same skeleton logic:

1. **Anchor deep.** The wire runs up through the stem and at least a third of the way into the calyx — never just glued to the surface. Glue-on stems snap at the first bend.
2. **Tape under tension.** Floral tape only sticks when stretched. One finger-length of tape, pulled as it wraps, seals wire to paper invisibly.
3. **Branch before you dress.** Wire branches are joined with figure-eight wraps, not blobs of hot glue. Glue lumps show through paper.

In the [Banksia Armature — Large](/product/banksia-armature-large), the main stem, the leaves and even the individual banksia styles are all wired separately, then gathered. That is why the piece can be re-posed a year later without anything tearing.

## Why movement reads as real

Real flowers are never perfectly straight. They curve toward light, droop when heavy, and tremble slightly when a door slumps past them. Wire lets you copy that:

- **Bend the stem 5mm off straight** — eyes read immediate imperfection as organic.
- **Counter-lean the bloom** — head forward, stem back. This single move sells more bouquets than any petal detail.
- **Weight the head** — a dense bloom should pull its stem into a gentle arc, exactly like a real rose after rain.

Our [Everlasting Native Bundle — Table Centre](/product/native-bundle-table-centre) is built almost entirely on an armature base, which is why it sits low and stable on a reception desk but still looks like it just grew there.

## Perth-proofing the structure

Perth summers hit 40°C and homes run aircon — both dry environments where bare paper edges get brittle. The wire takes the mechanical stress so the paper does not: when a piece gets knocked, the armature flexes and the paper stays still. Add galvanised wire in our climate and nothing rusts through the tape.

The one rule: **never leave wire ends exposed.** A raw cut end will eventually wick moisture. Every terminal gets folded back or capped in glue.

## From idea to bloom

A new sculpture starts as a wire sketch — literally pliers and wire before any paper is cut. I pose the skeleton on the bench, photograph it, and only then work out the paper. The bloom dresses the armature, not the other way round.

If you want to feel the difference in your hands, the [Blush Rose Bouquet](/product/eucalyptus-paper-rose-bouquet-blush) ships with fully wired stems — take one out of the wrapping and bend it. It will hold exactly where you put it. That is the wire talking.
