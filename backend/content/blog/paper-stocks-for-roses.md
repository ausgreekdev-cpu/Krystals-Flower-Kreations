## Why paper choice decides everything

I have made thousands of paper roses in my Perth studio, and the difference between a bloom that looks almost real and one that looks like a craft project almost always comes down to the paper. Petal edges, how light passes through the flower, whether a curled tip springs back or flops over — none of that is glue technique. It is stock choice.

These are the five papers I actually buy, what each one is good for, and the cut settings I run on my Cricut Maker so you are not guessing.

## 1. Canson 65lb textured cardstock — my everyday rose

This is what goes into the [Eucalyptus Paper Rose Bouquet](/product/eucalyptus-paper-rose-bouquet-blush). It is around 190gsm with a fine linen texture that catches light exactly like a real petal does. The blush and sage shades are pre-coloured right through the sheet, so a cut edge never shows white.

- **Best for:** rose and peony petals, everyday bouquets
- **Cost:** roughly $0.85 a sheet bought in packs
- **Watch out for:** it holds a curl beautifully, but deep folds can leave a faint crease line on very dark colours

## 2. Colorplan 175gsm — the crisp one

Colorplan is smoother and slightly cooler to handle than Canson. It cuts razor-clean, which matters on tiny petals and pointed lily shapes. I reach for it when a customer wants something modern and graphic rather than soft and romantic.

- **Best for:** sharp silhouettes, statement singles
- **Cost:** the premium option — expect double the Canson price
- **Watch out for:** smooth faces can look flat in photos if you do not shape them

## 3. Canson Mi-Teintes 160gsm — the veined one

The back of every Mi-Teintes sheet has a subtle embossed vein pattern. On a peony or a garden rose it reads as natural petal texture with zero extra work. At 160gsm it is light enough to ruffle the edges with a bone folder.

- **Best for:** large open blooms, peonies, garden roses
- **Cost:** mid-range, one sheet goes a long way
- **Watch out for:** the veining runs one way — keep it consistent across a bloom or the petals look mismatched

## 4. Quill smooth 220gsm — the sturdy budget pick

Available at most Perth newsagents, Quill smooth is heavier and holds structure in hot weather. It is my pick for flowers that live in a vase without water in a warm room. It does not mimic a real petal as closely, but it survives handling.

- **Best for:** bridesmaid posies kids will touch, market stall stock
- **Cost:** cheapest per sheet if you buy multipacks
- **Watch out for:** heavy cards need a slower mat pass — see the cut settings below

## 5. Crepe paper, 140g double grave — for stretch

Traditional Italian crepe stretches, which lets you cup a petal with your thumbs instead of only curling it. Nothing else makes ranunculus or a ruffled carnation convincingly. I use it in small doses for accent blooms in otherwise cardstock bouquets.

- **Best for:** ranunculus, carnations, ruffled fillers
- **Cost:** inexpensive per roll, wildly efficient per bloom
- **Watch out for:** crepe fades in direct sun — keep it out of that west-facing Perth window

## Cricut cut settings I actually use

- **Canson 65lb / Colorplan 175:** Cardstock (heavy), blade 4, pressure more, **two passes** for anything under 3cm
- **Mi-Teintes 160:** Cardstock, blade 3, pressure default, check the small pieces before lifting
- **Quill 220:** Fabric-gel or heavy cardstock, blade 5, pressure more — slow and deep beats fast and shallow
- **Crepe:** Light cardstock, blade 2, pressure less, and **mirror your mat** so the grain runs the way you want

If petals start snagging, the blade is dull, not the setting. I swap mine every few market batches.

Start with Canson 65lb. It forgives mistakes, curls predictably, and comes in the colours Perth brides ask for most.
